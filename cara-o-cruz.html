Vicente: ¿Cara o Cruz?

Vicente is finishing the Master and his first salary depends on a coin toss. You choose for Vicente again. Four choices reveal your risk aversion (σ) and show why, with a concave utility, a gamble is always worth less than its average. That is Jensen's inequality.

Lab 1 · Jensen's inequality

The curve, the chord and the gap

Vicente earns L = 12,000 − Δ with cruz and H = 12,000 + Δ with cara, where cara comes up with probability p. The chord between u(L) and u(H) gives expected utility E[u(c)]. The curve above the average gives u(E[c]). Jensen's inequality says that when u is concave, the chord lies below the curve: E[u(c)] ≤ u(E[c]).

Risk aversion and Jensen's inequality

Jensen's inequality
u concave ⟹ E[u(c)] ≤ u(E[c])

For a concave function, the average of the function lies below the function of the average. Geometrically, the chord between two points lies below the curve. With utility, a gamble gives less utility than receiving its average for sure. Risk aversion is concavity of u. The inequality flips for convex u (risk lovers) and becomes an equality for linear u (risk neutrality).

Certainty equivalent
u(CE) = E[u(c)],   π = E[c] − CE

The certainty equivalent is the sure income valued exactly like the gamble. The risk premium π is what Vicente would give up to get rid of the risk. In Rounds A–C you choose CE directly, and the page inverts it to find your σ.

Arrow–Pratt
ARA = −u″(c)/u′(c),   RRA = −c u″(c)/u′(c),   π ≈ ½ · ARA · Var(c)

The premium for a small risk is proportional to its variance and to the curvature of u, measured by absolute risk aversion. Multiplying by c gives relative risk aversion, which is unit-free. For CRRA, RRA = σ at every income level.

CRRA vs CARA
CRRA: CE(λL, λH) = λ · CE(L, H);   CARA u = −e−ac: π depends only on the gamble, not on income

Under CRRA, scaling every outcome by λ scales the certainty equivalent and the risk premium by λ. Round C doubles every amount in Round A, so CRRA predicts your premium exactly doubles. Under CARA it rises by more than double, because absolute risk aversion stays fixed while the variance quadruples: between 2 and 4 times, about 3 times for a CARA consumer as risk averse as a σ = 2 CRRA consumer at €12,000.

El Gordo
E[prize] = €14 < €20 ticket ⟹ E[u(w + X)] ≤ u(w + E X) < u(w)

A décimo of the Lotería de Navidad is a gamble with negative expected value: 70% of ticket sales come back as prizes. Jensen's inequality plus monotonicity means no risk-averse or risk-neutral expected-utility maximiser buys one. Yet millions of Spaniards do. Friedman and Savage (1948) explained it with a utility function that is convex over large gains. Others point to the pleasure of the draw on 22 December, or not wanting to be the only one in the office without the winning number.

One σ, two jobs
CRRA: RRA = σ = 1/EIS

CRRA uses one parameter for two different attitudes: dislike of risk across states (this game) and dislike of fluctuations across time (Game 1). Data usually want them apart, for example high risk aversion for the equity premium and a moderate EIS for saving. Epstein–Zin (1989) preferences separate them (L18).

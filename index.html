Vicente en Alicante

An alien has landed on the UA campus in San Vicente del Raspeig to do the two-year Master. The locals call him Vicente, after the town. You decide how Vicente splits the money between year 1 and year 2. Three choices, at three different interest rates, reveal how patient you are (β) and how willing you are to shift consumption over time (the EIS).

Euler lab

One euro, two years

Vicente can move one euro from year 1 to year 2. Giving it up today costs u′(c1) in utility. Next year it becomes R euros, each worth u′(c2), discounted by β. Vicente is at the optimum only when the two sides balance. That balance is the Euler equation.

u′(c1) = β R u′(c2)   ⟺   c2/c1 = (βR)1/σ   (CRRA)

Chuleta

The five ideas behind the game

CRRA utility
u(c) = (c1−σ − 1)/(1 − σ),   σ > 0;   σ → 1 gives u(c) = ln c

One parameter, σ, sets the curvature: how fast the joy of an extra euro dies out as Vicente consumes more. It is called constant relative risk aversion because −c u″(c)/u′(c) = σ at every level of c. Scale all of Vicente's budgets by ten and every choice scales by ten too: the shares spent in each year do not change. That is why balanced growth paths exist with CRRA.

Discount factor
U = u(c1) + β u(c2),   β = 1/(1+ρ)

β is how much one util next year is worth today. In Round 1 the interest rate is zero, so any tilt in Vicente's consumption comes only from β: impatient students tilt toward year 1. Quarterly macro models use β ≈ 0.99, annual ones β ≈ 0.96.

EIS
EIS = d ln(c2/c1) / d ln R = 1/σ

The elasticity of intertemporal substitution measures how much consumption growth reacts to the interest rate. Rounds 2 and 3 change R; the slope of your ln(c2/c1) against ln R is your EIS. Under CRRA, the EIS is pinned down by the same σ that governs risk aversion. Game 2 tests whether that holds for you.

Euler equation
u′(ct) = β Rt+1 Et[u′(ct+1)]

The first-order condition of the saving problem: the utility cost of saving one euro equals the discounted utility of what it buys next period. It holds for any concave u. With CRRA and no uncertainty it becomes c2/c1 = (βR)1/σ; take logs to get Δ ln c ≈ σ−1(r − ρ). This is the same equation that sits in the NGM (L1), the RBC model (L11) and asset pricing (L18).

Risk aversion & Jensen
RRA = −c u″/u′ = σ,   u concave ⟹ E[u(c)] ≤ u(E[c])

These live in the second game, Vicente: ¿Cara o Cruz?, where Vicente's year-2 income depends on a coin toss.

Why the game works. With CRRA, ln(c2/c1) = EIS·ln β + EIS·ln R. That is a straight line in ln R. Round 1 (R = 1) gives the intercept, Rounds 2 and 3 give the slope. With log utility (σ = 1), Round 1 alone gives β = c2/c1.
